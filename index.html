How to write strong testimony (read first)
  • Professional, but personal. Address the committee respectfully and use plain words, but write like yourself. Your own experience is what lawmakers can’t get from lobbyists.
  • Be specific. “I drive 45 minutes each way” beats “this hurts rural families.” Use real numbers, places, and dates.
  • Cite your sources. Name where every statistic, study, or law comes from. Never guess at a number. If you can’t source it, say it’s your experience (“in my classroom, I see…”).
  • One story, told briefly. Testimony is public record. Share only what you’re comfortable seeing published, and leave out children’s names, your street address, and private medical details.
  • Stay on this bill. Refer to it by number, criticize the policy rather than people, and make one clear ask.
  • Use your own voice. Talking points are a starting point. Rewrite them; copied language gets noticed.

Bill & committee

Prefilled from the call to action. Check these against the committee’s page.

1. Who are you?

Examples: a parent, student, small business owner, healthcare worker, renter, patient, retiree, advocate.

2. Why are you connected to this issue?

Completes: “I am writing today because…”. How does it affect your family, job, school, health, finances, or community?

3. Your position

One clear reason, backed by a fact or experience. If you use a number or study, add it under “Cite your sources.”

4. What would this bill mean for you?

Be specific. “My family will drive 45 minutes farther” beats “This hurts families.” Try: If this bill becomes law, I will have to… my community will…

5. Tell one story

What happened, who was affected, what did it cost in time, money, safety, health, or stress?

6. Is there a better way? (optional)

If you support the bill, you can leave this blank.

7. Questions for lawmakers (optional)

One per line. Who enforces it? Who pays? Who could be unintentionally harmed? What evidence shows it works? (Left out of oral testimony to save time.)

8. What if lawmakers make the opposite choice? (optional)

Cite your sources (optional, encouraged)

One per line: organization or author, title, year, and a link if you have one. Written testimony lists them at the end; oral testimony offers to share them.
